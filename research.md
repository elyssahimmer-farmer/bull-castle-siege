# Castle siege (Lion vs Bull): research

**Scope.** This is an invented siege between two made-up armies. The **Lion banner** besieges and the **Bull banner** holds the castle (NOTES.md, Round 1). Every physical detail should still come from real castles, sieges and soldiers. The castle is modelled on **Bodiam Castle** (licence to crenellate 1385). The knights wear **plate armour of c. 1380–1420**. Everything else uses **c. 1380–1420** as its reference date. The one exception is the archers and spear soldiers: they are dressed like reference picture 12 (mail, conical nasal helmets, kite shields), an earlier look the family chose on purpose (NOTES.md, Round 2). The Lions also bring **ten cannon** (DESIGN.md decision 10; see §16).

**How this was made.** Every claim below was read in a source during this session, through a web-fetch tool that summarises pages. Nothing comes from memory. Where a fact only came back through a search-engine summary, and the page itself was not read, it is marked **(relayed)**. Treat relayed facts as leads, not as confirmed. Quotations were returned as verbatim fragments. Before any of them goes onto a label, check it once against the page or scan (see Gaps).

**Layers used:**
- **[text]**: a primary source of the period says it (a chronicle, a record, a medieval treatise, or a surviving object).
- **[historian]**: a museum, a heritage body, an archaeological survey, a modern historian, or experimental archaeology says it.
- **[tradition/art]**: commonly assumed, painted or re-enacted, but not in a source read here.
- **[silent]**: no source read here says it.
- **[INVENTED]**: the scene will have to make it up. For an invented siege that covers a lot, including both armies, every name and the course of the fight.

  - A Bull garrison of a few dozen on the walls, with a sally of 10 to 20 riders, is in proportion to the sources.
- **Women in the defence.** These are attested and gentle, so they would make good tap moments:
  - At Hennebont the countess "ordered the ladies and other women to unpave the streets, carry the stones to the ramparts" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 106].

## 3. The place: Bodiam Castle as the model

### What is solid
- **Date and builder.** Sir Edward Dalyngrigge received a licence to crenellate on 20 or 21 October 1385. The castle was built in one phase and probably finished before 1392 [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray 2004 pp. 13–14, 59; Historic England schedule via Gatehouse]. The licence text has him "strengthen with a wall of stone and lime, and crenellate ... for the defence of the adjacent country" [text: Patent Rolls, as quoted in Thackray p. 59 via Wikipedia].
- **Plan.**
  - A square castle with no keep, rising straight out of the moat "without a Berm" [historian: Northwestern *Medieval Buildings*]. It has a hollow courtyard ringed by domestic ranges [historian: Historic England listing via britishlistedbuildings].
  - **Four round corner towers**, plus **square towers** in the middle of the east and west walls, the gatehouse in the north wall and the postern tower in the south [historian: Gatehouse; Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 32–33].
  - The corner towers are "not completely round" and meet the walls in two different, irregular ways [historian: Northwestern].
- **Island size.** The castle island measures **52 × 56 m over the projecting towers** [historian: RCHME survey 1988, via NT HER MNA128092]. This is the best measured figure found. Build the castle footprint to it.
- **Storeys.** The curtain walls and ranges are **two storeys** over basements. The towers and gatehouse are **three storeys** and stand taller than the curtain [historian: Wikipedia citing Allen Brown 2004 p. 99; Gatehouse; listing].
- **Battlements.** Every tower and the curtain are crenellated [historian: Wikipedia lead; photo 3]. The gatehouse has "a corbelled, machicolated and crenellated parapet" [historian: Gatehouse, from the Historic England schedule].
- **Machicolations.** Only the top of the gatehouse is machicolated [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 36–37].
- **Loops.** Bodiam has **gunloops**, not arrow loops, and only in the gatehouse. They are keyhole gunports "for hand-held cannons", at ground level [historian: Wikipedia citing Cathcart King 1988 p. 150; NT; Historic UK; Gatehouse]. The rest of the curtain is pierced by "simple lancet windows" [historian: Gatehouse]. Its walls were "relatively thin and contained a number of large mullioned windows" [historian: Sussex Castles]. Arrow loops are [silent] for Bodiam in every source read.
- **Gatehouse.**
  - Twin towers, three storeys [historian: Wikipedia].
  - A vaulted passage whose ring-bosses are "murder holes" for dropping missiles [historian: NT; Wikipedia citing McNeill 1992 pp. 98–99].
  - Guard rooms with a basement [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 36–37].
  - Three coats of arms over the gate and Dalyngrigge's unicorn-head crest [historian: Wikipedia; NT].
- **Portcullis.**
  - The passage originally had **three** portcullises [historian: Wikipedia citing Cathcart King p. 150; NT].
  - The outermost survives, "iron-clad oak ... thought to be one of the oldest in England" [historian: NT].
  - The barbican's north gate also had a portcullis slot [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray p. 35].
- **Barbican.** A two-storey outer gate between the Octagon and the gatehouse, with a vaulted passage. Only a piece of its west wall survives [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray p. 35; Gatehouse].
- **Bridges and approach.** These matter for the ram.
  - In Dalyngrigge's time visitors came "from the west over an L-shaped bridge" [historian: NT].
  - One bridge ran from the west bank to an octagonal island, the **Octagon**, and another from the Octagon to the main entrance. The **west-bank section was a drawbridge**; the rest was fixed [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 33–35].
  - Gatehouse adds that the gatehouse "was originally connected to the barbican by a drawbridge" (relayed by search).
  - The bridge "was angled across the moat, leaving any attackers exposed" [historian: Historic UK].
  - Today a stone causeway runs from the gate halfway into the north moat [historian: Gatehouse].
  - The medieval bridge timbers still lie on the moat bed [historian: NT]. The bridge footings were found in 1919–20 and 1970 [historian: Gatehouse, relayed].
  - The **postern** in the south tower reached the south bank by a drawbridge and a long timber bridge [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 33–35].
- **Moat.**
  - Roughly rectangular, about **115 × 155 m**, oriented almost north–south, about 1.5 ha including the island [historian: RCHME 1988 via NT HER].
  - Its depth is disputed (see Disagreements). Fed by springs [historian: NT HER; Wikipedia citing Thackray p. 55].
  - Built at the tip of a sandstone spur, with a 3 m cut scarp on the west and dams on the south and east [historian: RCHME via NT HER].
  - "It has been suggested that the moat could have been drained in a day" [historian: Saul 1995, via Wikipedia].
  - **Derived, not sourced:** the castle island is about 52–56 m across in a 115 × 155 m moat. That leaves roughly 30 m of water east and west and roughly 50 m north and south [INVENTED: arithmetic from the two RCHME figures, assuming the island is centred, which the Northwestern site says it "almost" is].
- **Stone.** Sandstone ashlar from Wadhurst, about 15 km to the north-west [historian: listing; Gatehouse].
- **Landscape.**
  - Mill pond, ponds and watery approaches, and a "Gun Garden" earthwork to the north [historian: Gatehouse; Northwestern].
  - Liddiard reads the approach as deliberately indirect, made to show off the castle [historian: Wikipedia citing Liddiard 2005 pp. 8–10].
  - The River Rother lies 250 m to the south [historian: Gatehouse].
- **Hoardings.** These are timber galleries built out over the battlements. No source read mentions them at Bodiam [silent]. Bodiam's gatehouse has stone machicolations instead.
- **A real gun from Bodiam's moat.** A bombard with a bore of about 12 in (30 cm) was found when the moat was drained (see §16) [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard (weapon)*].

### Was Bodiam a "real" fortress? Historians disagree
- Saul and Coulson read the moat and walls as largely for show ("partly defensive and partly for show": NT). The walls are "relatively thin" with big windows [historian: Sussex Castles; Wikipedia citing Cathcart King p. 150].
- **Its only recorded siege** is 1483. The Earl of Surrey was ordered to besiege it, and it is "not recorded whether the siege went ahead". Lewknor probably "surrendered without much resistance" [historian: Wikipedia citing Thackray pp. 21–24].
- **For the scene:** Bodiam is a fine model for the look. Nothing here says it ever withstood a bombardment, so the battle itself is [INVENTED].

### Dimensions not found (see Gaps)
Curtain-wall height and thickness, tower diameter and height, and gatehouse height and width are not given by any authoritative source read.

## 4. Clothing and props

### 4a. Knights: plate armour c. 1380–1420 (the family's choice, picture 14)
- **Pieces of a full harness.** The list: helmet; gorget or bevor; spaulders or pauldrons; besagews (rondels); rerebraces, couters and vambraces; gauntlets; cuirass (breast- and backplate) with fauld, tassets and culet; mail skirt; cuisses, poleyns, greaves and sabatons [historian: Wikipedia *Plate armour*].
  - "By about 1420, complete suits of plate armour had been developed" [historian: same].
  - Mail was worn under the plate at the weak points [historian: same].
  - Nothing complete survives from c. 1400. The Met's "c. 1400" armour is assembled from pieces found at Chalcis (found 1840) to show "a full harness of the c. 1400 style" [historian: Met 29.154.3, relayed by search].
- **Helmet: the visored bascinet.**
  - A pointed skull with a mail **aventail** hanging from staples (*vervelles*) round its lower edge, covering the neck and shoulders. The aventail appears from about 1330 [historian: myArmoury].
  - From about 1380 the visor was drawn out into a conical "muzzle". "Pig-face" and "hounskull" are modern names; people then just said "visored bascinets" [historian: myArmoury; Blair, via search].
  - Eye slits are about 8 mm wide, with breathing holes mostly on the right [historian: myArmoury].
  - Around 1400 the back of the skull ran almost straight down [historian: myArmoury].
  - Plate gorgets began replacing the aventail from about 1400–1410 [historian: relayed by search, citing a myArmoury-type source].
  - Surviving examples include the Royal Armouries' North Italian bascinet IV.470 (c. 1380–1400) and the de Melsa bascinet (c. 1390–1400) [historian: relayed by search].
- **Jupon.**
  - The long surcoat gave way by the mid-14th century to the **jupon**, "a much shorter item and ... often padded" [historian: Wikipedia *Jupon*].
  - The "surcoatless period" runs from about 1420 to 1485 [historian: same]. So c. 1380–1420 is exactly when a knight wore his **arms on a short, tight, padded jupon over his plate**.
  - The best surviving example is the **Black Prince's jupon** at Canterbury (d. 1376). It is padded linen with velvet panels in alternating red and blue: gilt lions on the red, gilt fleurs-de-lis on the blue. It laces at the front [historian: Textile Research Centre Leiden, citing Janet Arnold 1993; relayed by search].
- **"Shiny" plate.** The family asked for it. The sources read do not say when bare polished "white harness" became usual. The jupon over the body means a knight of c. 1400 showed bright steel on his **arms, legs and helmet**, while his chest and belly wore his arms in cloth [historian: the jupon period above; the bare-body point is inferred].
  - **Design note:** cover the torso with a Lion or Bull jupon and keep the limbs and bascinet shining. That keeps the "shiny knight" and is right for the date.
- **Weight and movement: sources disagree.**
  - "Around 15–25 kg" for a complete suit, and "the wearer remained highly agile" [historian: Wikipedia *Plate armour*].
  - Replicas of *mid- to late-15th-century* field armour averaged **35 ± 5 kg** with the arming doublet. Walking in it cost **2.1–2.3×** the energy of walking unloaded, and breathing was impaired [historian: Askew, Formenti & Minetti 2012, PMC3248716].
  - **For animation:** knights walk and run freely but tire. Avoid leaping or acrobatics.
- **Lance.**
  - War lances were made of strong wood such as ash or oak, with sharp heads. The vamplate (hand guard) was "in use by the late 14th century". Lances were couched underarm in a closed-rank charge [historian: Wikipedia *Lance*].
  - No length is given for the war lance. Jousting lances average about 3 m [historian: same].
- **Pennon on the lance.** "Flown on his own lance as a personal ensign": a small pointed or swallow-tailed flag [historian: Boutell 1914 via Wikipedia *Heraldic flag*].
- **Horse.**
  - The **caparison** is a cloth covering "frequently embroidered with the coat of arms of the horse's rider", worn in battle and in tournaments [historian: Wikipedia *Caparison*]. Re-enactment tests found it could lessen arrow damage [historian: same].
  - Pictures 14 and 15 show this.
  - Plate horse armour (the chanfron) is [silent] in what was read.

### 4b. Archers and spear soldiers: the picture 12 look (the family's earlier-style choice)
- **What picture 12 shows** (looked at in `.scratch/refs/pictures-1.jpg`):
  - conical steel helmets with nasal bars;
  - mail shirts over coloured tunics;
  - long **kite shields** painted in strong colours with bold beasts and crosses: red with a white beast, yellow and green;
  - spears.
  The Commons page calls it "Norman infantry with with Kite shield (xi century)". Author Battlelight, CC BY-SA 3.0, uploaded 1 June 2014. It is categorised under Viking re-enactment [historian: Commons file page].
- **Kite shield.**
  - Popular in Western Europe "during the 1000s" and later replaced by the smaller "heater" shield [historian: Wikipedia *Kite shield*].
  - **So in 1380–1420 it is about two centuries out of date.** The family chose this on purpose. Say so in DESIGN.md's last section.
  - Made of laminated wood, hide and iron, "at least three to five feet high" (Wikipedia; it seems large, so treat it as an upper bound).
  - Held by **enarmes**, forearm straps that "gripped the shield tight to the arm".
  - Some had a **guige**, a longer strap so it could be "slung over one shoulder when not in use".
  - Byzantine infantry "frequently carried kite shields on their backs while on the march, sometimes upside down" [historian: same].
  - **Archers with shields slung on their backs (the family's request):** the guige makes this physically right. No source read shows *archers* carrying them so [silent]. In the Bayeux Tapestry most English fight on foot with kite shields [historian: Wikipedia]. Which panels show slung shields was not confirmed (see Gaps).
- **Archers of c. 1400 (for contrast).** The period-correct archer wore no kite shield. Wikipedia *English longbow* does not cover archers' own armour [silent].

### 4c. Bows, arrows, crossbows, pavises
- **Longbow (Mary Rose, 1545: the best physical evidence, about 130 years after the reference date):**
  - 172 bows recovered. Lengths **1,839–2,113 mm**, a single piece of **yew**, D-shaped, about 35 × 33 mm at the centre.
  - Draw weights estimated at **65–175 lb**, peaking at 110 lb [historian: Mary Rose Trust].
  - Arrows **667–880 mm**, mostly poplar, fletched with goose or swan feathers. Draw length 28–30 in.
  - Leather wrist guards; horn nocks [historian: Mary Rose Trust].
- **How arrows were carried.**
  - Archers "stuck their arrows upright in the ground at their feet". A battle issue was **60–72 arrows**, and boys ran more arrows up [historian: Wikipedia *English longbow*].
  - On the Mary Rose, parts of one **arrow bag**, with evidence for 17 more [historian: Mary Rose Trust].
  - Hip quivers are [silent] in the sources read.
  - **Design note:** an arrow bag at the belt plus a sheaf stuck in the ground is the sourced look.
- **Range.**
  - Flight range about 370 m for a professional in Edward III's time (Oakeshott). A 1542 minimum practice range of 200 m.
  - A replica 150 lbf bow sent a 1.89 oz arrow 359 yd.
  - Heavy arrows were still dangerous at 240 yd (Strickland & Hardy) [historian: Wikipedia *English longbow*].
- **Rate.** Sources disagree: about **6 a minute** with the heaviest bows, versus "twelve or more" [historian: Wikipedia *English longbow* and *Crossbow*].
- **Volleys.** At Crécy the English "let fly their arrows so wholly [together] and so thick, that it seemed snow" [text: Froissart (Berners)].
- **Crossbow c. 1400.**
  - Composite prods from the 13th century; **steel prods** "around the 14th century" [historian: Wikipedia *Crossbow*].
  - Spanned with a stirrup and belt hook, or from the 14th century a goat's-foot lever, windlass or cranequin.
  - About **two bolts a minute** [historian: same].
  - Bolts are short and heavy, with a four-sided "quarrel" head [historian: same].
- **Pavise.**
  - A large standing shield that a crossbowman sheltered behind while spanning [historian: Wikipedia *Crossbow*].
  - At Crécy the Genoese pavises had been left in the baggage wagons [historian: Wikipedia *Genoese crossbowmen*, relayed by search].
  - DeVries, *Arms & Armour* 4.2 (2007), is the scholarly study. Only its abstract was readable.
- **Shooting from walls.**
  - Carlisle's attackers "shot their arrows thickly to prevent anyone showing his head above the wall". The defenders answered with "such dense volleys of darts and arrows, likewise stones" [text: Lanercost].
  - Hennebont townsmen lined the ditch to shoot [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 108].
  - The gatehouse parapet "could have concealed archers" [historian: NT].
  - At Bodiam, archers would shoot from the **crenels** (the gaps in the battlements) and the machicolations. The loops are for guns (§3).
- **Springalds.** Carlisle's defenders had "seven or eight similar machines ... which are called springalds, for discharging long darts" [text: Lanercost]. Picture 9 (Tower of London) shows a springald.

### 4d. Spear soldiers and the shield wall
- **Shoulder to shoulder.** Soldiers stood "shoulder to shoulder and holding their shields so that they would abut or overlap" [historian: Wikipedia *Shield wall*].
  - Pushing with shields used "the weight of numbers". Once breached, "the whole formation tended to fall apart quickly" [historian: same; several claims there are tagged "citation needed"].
- **Schiltron** (the Scottish spear formation of 1298–1322):
  - Front rank kneeling with spear butts in the earth, rear ranks levelling spears over their heads, "twelve foot spears" [historian: Oman via Wikipedia *Schiltron*].
  - At Bannockburn men had "shields closely locked in front of them" (Trokelowe) and came on "like a thick-set hedge" (*Vita Edwardi Secundi*) [text, via Wikipedia].

## 5. Heraldry: the Lion and the Bull

- **Tinctures** [text: Fox-Davies ch. VII]:
  - **Metals:** "The metals are gold and silver, these being termed "or" and "argent"". Gold is painted yellow or gold. Silver is left white or painted "Chinese white".
  - **Colours:** "gules (red), azure (blue), vert (green), purpure (purple)", and sable (black).
  - **The rule:** "colour cannot be placed upon colour, nor metal upon metal". A Lion or a Bull must be a metal on a colour field, or a colour on a metal field.
  - "Most ancient coats of arms were simple" [text: Fox-Davies ch. VII].
- **The lion rampant** [text: Fox-Davies ch. XI]:
  - "depicted in profile, and erect, resting upon its sinister hind-paw";
  - early artists tried "to fill up as large a proportion of the space available as was possible";
  - claws and tongue: "If it is not itself gules, its tongue and claws are usually represented as of that colour, unless the lion be on a field of gules". Then they are blue: "armed and langued" azure;
  - in the later 14th and 15th century (our period), "the lion's body is no longer placed like a pillar", and "the jaws grow deep and are widely opened";
  - the tail: "adorned with flame-like bunches of hair, strikes outwards";
  - the paws: "each end in three knobs, similar to a clover-leaf, out of which the claws come forth".
  - **Real lions to avoid copying:**
    - England's lions are "three lions passant guardant" (walking, facing the viewer) [text: Fox-Davies ch. XI].
    - A red lion rampant on gold is Scotland's [silent: not confirmed in the pages read; the chapter section read didn't give the blazon]. Avoid red-on-gold to be safe.
    - Picture 16 (Codex Manesse) shows lions on knights' shields and caparisons.
- **The bull** [text: Fox-Davies ch. XII]:
  - Bulls appear **rampant** and **passant** (walking, one forefoot raised), and their heads appear "caboshed" (full face, no neck).
  - "A bull or cow is termed 'armed' if the horns are of a different tincture from the head". Hoofs are "unguled". A ring in the nose is "ringed".
  - Bulls "can never be represented proper" (in natural colour), except the Nevill family's pied bulls.
  - **Real bulls to avoid copying:** Ashley, "three bulls passant sable"; Nevill pied bulls; De Hasting, a bull salient [text: Fox-Davies ch. XII].
- **Suggested invented arms** [INVENTED: check them with the family]:
  - **Lion:** *Azure, a lion rampant or, armed and langued gules*. A gold lion on blue, with red claws and tongue.
  - **Bull:** *Gules, a bull passant argent, armed or*. A white bull walking on red, with gold horns. (Or *Vert, a bull passant or*.)
  - Both obey the tincture rule. Neither matches a coat named in the pages read. Check against a roll of arms before final use.
- **Flag shapes** [historian: Boutell 1914 via Wikipedia *Heraldic flag*]:
  - **Banner:** square or oblong, "bearing the entire coat of arms of the owner". Before 1906 it was reserved for noblemen and **knights banneret**. This is the lord's flag: fly it over the Bull gatehouse and above the Lion commander.
  - **Standard:** appeared "around the mid-14th century". It is long and tapering, with a rounded or swallow-tailed fly. It carries badges in the **livery colours** rather than the full arms, and the English form has the **Cross of St George by the staff**. This is a gathering flag for a lord's men.
  - **Pennon:** a small pointed or swallow-tailed flag on a knight's own lance, with his badge or arms.
  - At Crécy every lord came "under his own banner and pennon" [text: Froissart (Berners)]. At Auberoche the French "displayed their banners and pennons" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVII].
- **Liveries.** Since standards were in livery colours, Lion soldiers could wear tunics in one livery pair and Bull soldiers in another [historian: Boutell via Wikipedia]. In picture 12 the colours are the shields'.

## 6. Timeline of mechanics (how each machine works)

### 6a. The counterweight trebuchet
- **Primary instructions (Villard, c. 1250).**
  - "If you want to make the strong engine which is called a trebuchet, pay close attention here."
  - "Here in front are the two windlasses and the double rope with which one draws back the beam".
  - "the counter-poise is very heavy, being a hopper full of earth" [text: Villard, trans. Contamine].
  - The hopper is paraphrased as about two large toises long, nine feet across and twelve feet deep [same page, paraphrase].
- **Arm ratio.**
  - Marino Sanudo (c. 1320) recommended a beam ratio of **1 : 5.5**, or 1 : 6 for long range [text: Sanudo, via Vemming Hansen 1992].
  - Beams were up to about **15 m**, with the throwing side 4–6 times the counterweight side [historian: Saimre 2007 via Wikipedia *Trebuchet*].
- **Fixed vs hinged counterweight.** Giles of Rome (c. 1280) said hinged boxes threw further than fixed ones [text: Giles, via Fulton 2018 via Wikipedia]. Pictures 7 and 8 show hinged boxes.
- **Reconstruction 1 (Vemming Hansen 1992, Nykøbing; the basis of Warwick's machine):**
  - A frame of two standing triangles on a heavy beech base. The arm is two ash beams spliced and iron-banded. Wooden axles and wedges, not nails.
  - Built for 4,000 kg ballast but tested at **2,000 kg**.
  - **Sling 5 m**; the rope-net pouch lasted about 120 shots.
  - **Shot:** 15 kg cement balls, up to 47–50 kg.
  - **Longest shot 168 m**, with about 300 m projected for 4,000 kg. Peak height 60–80 m.
  - Side-to-side scatter only **2–3 m**.
  - **Crew 10–12**. The arm was hauled down with a pulley tackle (medieval pictures show capstans or a treadmill).
  - **Loading 5–6 minutes; about one shot every 10 minutes** [historian: Vemming Hansen, *Acta Archaeologica* 63 (1992)].
  - He judged the medieval hammer-blow release unsafe and used a simple trigger [same].
- **Reconstruction 2 (Middelaldercentret's large machine, oak):**
  - Ballast **15 t**, about 21 t loaded, built for up to 300 kg shot but used with **15–20 kg** stones.
  - **Four people in treadmill winches**, about **half an hour to load** with safety checks.
  - The sling lies in a **V-shaped trough** and slides out as the arm swings. One crew member holds the **trigger rope** [historian: Middelaldercentret].
- **Warwick Castle.**
  - **18 m** tall [historian: Birmingham World and others, relayed] or "19 meters" [historian: medieval.eu]. **22 tonnes** [both].
  - Rebuilt after 2005 to Danish drawings from Vemming's centre [historian: medieval.eu; relayed].
  - A 4.5 t counterweight of boulders, and a 13 kg stone thrown **228 m** for a Guinness record [historian: Coventry Telegraph, relayed; the page itself was behind a paywall redirect].
  - Crews of **15–45 men**, "no more than a couple of shots per hour" [historian: medieval.eu].
  - Picture 7 shows its two **treadwheels** in the base.
- **Big medieval figures** [historian: Chevedden 2000 p. 72, Purton 2009 pp. 382–385, via Wikipedia *Trebuchet*]:
  - a 15 t counterweight throwing 60 kg about 365 m;
  - 30 t throwing 100 kg about 400 m;
  - reconstructions with 6 t throwing 55 kg 320 m.
  - Stones of about **66 kg** were found at Acre (1291) [historian: Fulton 2018 pp. 297–300].
- **Used in our period?**
  - Yes, though fading. Trebuchets were "phased out around the mid-15th century" [historian: Turnbull 2001, Purton 2010, via Wikipedia].
  - Bodiam itself (1385) was built with **gunloops**, so early cannon were also in use [historian: Wikipedia; NT]. See §16.
  - At Karlštejn in 1422, the besiegers' "catapults or large slings appear to have done more damage than the guns" [historian: Lützow p. 137]. Engines and guns served side by side.
- **Loop sequence for the scene** (from Vemming and Middelaldercentret) [historian]:
  1. The crew walks the treadwheels (or heaves the capstan); the arm comes down and the counterweight box rises.
  2. The arm is caught on its catch.
  3. A stone goes into the sling pouch, which is laid in the trough.
  4. One person pulls the trigger rope.
  5. The box drops and swings. The arm whips up and the sling whirls out of the trough. The pouch opens near the top and the stone flies high (60–80 m), landing within a few metres of the line.
  6. Repeat. One shot every few minutes is the fastest believable pace.

### 6b. What one stone does to a wall
- **Stirling 1304** (Edward I's engines):
  - "And cause to be raised there thirteen great engines" (the OCR reads "englues").
  - "And cause the stones to pass walls and towers".
  - "They overthrow the battlements around," [text: Langtoft p. 357].
  - The great engine: "And this at its stroke broke down the entire wall." [text: Langtoft pp. 357–358]
  - The garrison's own engine failed: "The rod broke, afterwards it was of no use." [text: Langtoft p. 357]
  - The *Flores Historiarum* says one Warwolf stone went through two walls, "like an arrow flying through cloth" [text: Luard 1890 vol. 3 p. 319, **via Wikipedia**, not read].
- **Auberoche:** four machines in six days "had demolished all the roofs of the towers". The garrison hid in "the vaulted rooms on the ground floor" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVI].
- **Hennebont:** Lewis of Spain made progress "by battering and destroying the walls with his machines" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 107].
- **Carlisle 1315:** the Scots "cast great stones continually against the Caldew gate and against the wall, but they did little or no injury" [text: Lanercost].
- **Dunbar 1338:** the English engine "warpyt at the wall gret stanys / Bathe hard and hewy ... Bot that nane merryng to thame made". My gloss: they hurled great hard, heavy stones at the wall, but these did them no harm [text: Wyntoun VIII.xxxiii, p. 432].
- **Gunstones:** see §16.
- **Summary for the design** [historian synthesis of the texts above]:
  - What sources most often report is **battlements, tower roofs and buildings knocked down**.
  - A wall breached by a *single* stone is told only of the Warwolf, and the *Flores* version sounds like boasting.
  - Strong walls often shrugged stones off (Carlisle, Dunbar).
  - The family's scripted hit is still well grounded if it **knocks off merlons and the parapet**, leaving a ragged gap in the battlements that stays. A deep breach right through the curtain is the dramatic end of the range. Flag this choice to the family.

### 6c. The ram, the moat and the gate
- **Getting over the moat.**
  - At Carlisle the Scots made "great numbers of fascines of corn and herbage to fill the moat" and "long bridges of logs running upon wheels". Neither worked: the bridges "sank to the depths by their own weight" [text: Lanercost].
  - At Château Gaillard the French "first filled the ditch and broke through the palisade" [historian: Wikipedia].
  - **At Bodiam** the moat is about 30–50 m of water around an island with no berm (§3). A ram could only reach the gate along the **bridge from the west bank via the Octagon**, under shot from the gatehouse and barbican, and the drawbridge would be raised.
  - **Design options:** (a) the ram crosses the fixed bridge and the causeway to the barbican gate; (b) the crew lays a timber bridge over the raised drawbridge's gap first. Option (b) is [INVENTED], modelled on Carlisle's wheeled bridges.
- **The strike.** The beam hangs on ropes or chains and is swung back and forth by the crew inside the shed [historian: Wikipedia *Battering ram*; Vegetius IV.14].
- **Defenders' answers** (Vegetius IV.23, my translation of the Latin read):
  - "Some lower patchwork quilts and mattresses on ropes, and set them against the places where the ram strikes" (*centones et culcitas funibus chalant*).
  - "Others catch the rams in nooses and, with many men, drag them sideways off the wall" (*laqueis captos arietes ... in obliquum trahunt*).
  - "Many tie to ropes a toothed iron like a pair of tongs, which they call the *wolf*" (*lupum uocant*).
  - "Sometimes bases and marble columns are hurled from the walls and break the rams" [text: Vegetius IV.23].
- **Bodiam's own answers.**
  - Machicolations over the gate, murder holes in the passage vault, and three portcullises [historian: §3].
  - At Hennebont the women carried paving stones up to throw [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 106].
  - At Dunbar the defenders dropped stones on the sow, per Bower [historian: History with Beth, paraphrase]. See Frightening.

### 6d. The sally
- **Hennebont 1342 (Manny), the best model for "knights ride out against the engines"** [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. LXXXI, pp. 107–108]:
  1. The besiegers set "the largest machine ... as near the castle as possible", and it "never ceased from casting stones into the town".
  2. Manny and Sir Yves "sallied quietly out of one of the gates, taking with them three hundred archers".
  3. The archers shot so well "that those who guarded the machine fled".
  4. The men-at-arms "broke down and cut in pieces this large machine".
  5. The besiegers armed and mounted and "galloped after them like madmen".
  6. The sally fell back "in good order until they came to the castle ditch", where the knights "made a stand", and townsmen came out to line the ditch and shoot.
- **Hennebont, the countess:**
  - She "collected three hundred horsemen, sallied out at their head by another gate that was not attacked" and burned the empty camp.
  - The besiegers cried "Treason! Treason!"
  - She came back "with great triumph, and sounds of trumpets" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 106].
- **Auberoche:** when Derby's relief force struck the French camp at supper, Sir Frank van Halle and Sir John Lendal "sallied out of the fortress, made for the plain" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVII].
- **Design fit:** the family's beat "Bull knights sally and meet the Lion knights" matches Manny exactly. Riders go out of a side gate (Bodiam's postern, south), ride at the trebuchet or a gun battery, the crew runs, the engine is broken, Lion knights mount and chase, and the Bull riders turn and hold at the moat edge with archers covering from the walls.

### 6e. The end
- **Carlisle:** "the Scots marched off in confusion to their own country", "leaving behind them all their engines of war", because a relief army was coming [text: Lanercost].
- **Dunbar:** the siege was abandoned [historian: Wikipedia and others]. Salisbury's parting rhyme: "Come I are, [or] come I late, / I fand Annot at the yhate." [text: Wyntoun VIII.xxxiii, near p. 434]
- **Mont-Saint-Michel 1423–24:** the English reportedly left two great bombards, "Les Michelettes", behind after their failed siege [historian: Smith & Brown 1989, via Société de l'Oriflamme]. Abandoned guns are an attested end to a failed siege.
- **The family's ending** (the Lions still standing withdraw over the hill while the Bulls cheer on the walls) matches Carlisle closely. Leaving the Lion guns and engines behind matches Carlisle and Mont-Saint-Michel.

## 9. Frightening or graphic material (for children aged 8 to 10)

These sources contain the following:
1. **Auberoche:** the French caught the garrison's messenger, hung his letters round his neck, put him in an engine and threw him back into the castle. "The valet fell quite dead." [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVI] **Leave it out entirely.**
2. **Things thrown by trebuchets:** the Warwick page lists "prisoners, manure, dead animals or rotten corpses", plus fireballs [historian: medieval.eu]. **Leave it out.** Throw only stones.
3. **Dunbar:** Salisbury's knight was killed by an arrow through his mail and padded coat ("throw thr[e] fawld off awbyrchowne ... And the arow in the body") [text: Wyntoun]. Bower has the stones dropped on the sow "dashing the heads of many inside to pieces" [historian: History with Beth, quoting Bower]. **Leave out both.** The sow or ram can be broken and its crew run out unhurt.
4. **Auberoche:** the besiegers meant to kill everyone if there was no unconditional surrender [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVI]. **Leave it out.**
5. **Château Gaillard:** non-combatants put out of the castle and "trapped between the besiegers and the castle" until Philip fed them [historian: Wikipedia]. **Leave it out.**
6. **Hennebont:** the countess's raid burned the camp. The Johnes text says "without any loss, for there were only servants and boys" [text]. Tents burning is fine at a distance. Show no people on fire.
7. **Carlisle:** defenders threw attackers "to the ground with their ladders" [text: Lanercost]. Under the toy-soldier rule, show at most a slide down and a sit-down, or leave it out.
8. **Fire:** fireballs and burning are in several sources. Keep any fire to tents or a broken engine, small and far off.
9. **Cannon** (details in §16): stink-pots of filth thrown to spread disease at Karlštejn; guns bursting; a keep blown up by gunpowder; guns causing "much loss in men and horses"; Froissart's "all the devils in hell". Leave all of these out. Keep the booms soft in the sound mix.

**The family's rule (NOTES.md):** toy-soldier falls, no blood, no wounds, nothing stuck in anyone, nobody struck once down, no lingering close-ups. Arrows should land in shields, ground and timber, never in people. Gunstones and trebuchet stones should land only on stone, earth and water. This matches the sources' own emphasis on routs and retreats rather than killing: "medieval battles were more often won by causing an opponent to flee" [historian: DeVries abstract, Medievalists.net].

## 10. How it's read (historians' framing)
- Sieges were usually decided by **time, hunger and relief armies** more than by breaking walls:
  - Stirling surrendered after "three months and eight days", when food ran out [text: Langtoft].
  - Carlisle ended when relief approached [text: Lanercost].
  - Auberoche ended with a relief attack [text: Froissart].
  - Harfleur in 1415 agreed to surrender if no French army came, and yielded on 22 September [historian: Wikipedia *Siege of Harfleur*].
  The scene's compressed bombardment, ram, sally and withdrawal is a highlights reel. Say so in DESIGN.md's last section.
- Bodiam is read by several historians as more a status symbol than a fortress (§3).
- Early guns "affected morale more than they damaged buildings" until the early 15th century [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*]. Harfleur (1415) is the first English siege where they did serious damage [historian: Wikipedia *Siege of Harfleur*, citing Spencer 2017].
- Experimental archaeology (Vemming Hansen, Middelaldercentret, Warwick) is the main evidence for crew size, rate and range. Medieval texts rarely give numbers.

## 11. Popular image vs the sources (the "adaptation" layer)
- **"Catapult."** The family's word. The sources' machine for 1380–1420 is the counterweight **trebuchet** (Villard: "trebuchet"; Langtoft: "engine"). The torsion catapult is ancient [historian: Chevedden abstract].
- **Machine-gun archery.** Films show endless rapid fire. The sources give about 6 shots a minute with heavy bows, crossbows at about 2 a minute, and 60–72 arrows per archer per battle (§4c).
- **Shining knights in bare plate.** In c. 1400 knights wore cloth jupons with their arms over the body plate (§4a). Bare "white" body armour is mostly after 1420.
- **One rock, one hole.** The sources mostly show battlements and roofs knocked off, and walls shrugging stones off (§6b).
- **Kite shields with plate knights.** Two centuries apart (§4b). It is the family's deliberate choice.
- **Bodiam with arrow slits.** Its loops are gunloops; the rest are windows (§3).
- **Drawbridge right at the gate.** The sources disagree on where Bodiam's drawbridges were (§12).
- **Pirate-ship cannon on two big wheels.** Big siege guns of 1380–1420 lay in timber beds on the ground, and fired a few times a day (§16).

## 12. Sources disagree
- **Bodiam moat depth.**
  - "Approximately 2–2.5 m" (NT HER, citing Curzon 1926). Water "to about 2 m" (RCHME 1988).
  - On average about **1.5 m**, but 2.1 m in the south-east, with 0.9 m of silt removed (Thackray 2004 via Wikipedia).
- **Bodiam tower and wall height and wall thickness.**
  - Web sources give towers of "13 m" and "18 m". A travel column says walls are 5.5 m thick and 18 m high; others say 2 m, 8 ft or 9 ft thick.
  - All were **relayed by search from unauthoritative sites**. Use none until checked (see Gaps).
- **Bodiam drawbridges.**
  - The west-bank span was the drawbridge (Wikipedia citing Thackray).
  - The gatehouse–barbican link was a drawbridge (Gatehouse, relayed).
  - Both may be true, giving two drawbridges.
- **Bodiam portcullises.** Three in the gate passage (Cathcart King; NT), versus "two portcullises" (a gazetteer, relayed).
- **Warwick trebuchet.** 18 m or 19 m tall. Built 2005, or an earlier machine of 1999–2001 (relayed). Counterweight 4.5 t (reported) or about 5 t (a photo caption's recollection).
- **Warwolf.**
  - Its name: Langtoft has "Ludgar" in the French and "Lucigar" in Wright's English (p. 357); the Exchequer account has *Lupus Guerre*.
  - Whether it was used before the surrender: Bain vol. 2 p. 405 has the garrison surrender and Edward test the engine anyway (via Wikipedia).
  - Its workforce: "forty-nine" (Prestwich) versus "50" and others.
  - The "30 wagons" figure is tagged citation-needed.
- **Dunbar.** The siege start (13 January) and end (10 or 16 June 1338) vary by site. The sow and the "farrow" taunt are Bower's, not Wyntoun's.
- **Longbow rate.** About 6 a minute (modern war-bow archers) versus "twelve or more" (the Wikipedia *Crossbow* comparison).
- **Armour weight.** 15–25 kg (Wikipedia *Plate armour*, general) versus 30–50 kg for later 15th-century field armour (Askew et al. 2012).
- **Black Prince's jupon colours.** Red and blue panels (TRC Leiden, citing Arnold) versus dark blue or black (HistoryRise). The TRC account is the better source.
- **Kite shield size.** "At least three to five feet" (Wikipedia). It looks large; use about chin to knee as in picture 12.
- **Château Gaillard.** The family named it for trebuchets, but the page read names only "siege engines", belfries and **mining**. It was taken by mining and through the garrison's latrine chute, not by trebuchet. No primary source (William the Breton) was read for it.
- **Cannon disagreements:** see §16.

## 16. Early cannon, c. 1380–1420 (DESIGN.md decision 10: ten guns for the Lions)

### 16a. Were guns at sieges in our period? Yes
- **Bodiam was built for them.** Its gatehouse has keyhole gunloops "for hand-held cannons" (1385 onward) [historian: §3].
- **A bombard was found in Bodiam's own moat.** It has a bore of "approximately 12 in (30 cm)" [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard (weapon)*]. It is a hoop-and-stave muzzle-loader, "late 14th or very early 15th century", now at the Royal Artillery Museum, Woolwich, with a copy shown at the castle (relayed by search). One account suggests it was dumped in the moat after an abortive siege in the Wars of the Roses (relayed).
- **Froissart, 1370s–1380s.**
  - At Pont-Audemer the French "had with them many cannon, and various engines and machines" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CCCXXX, pp. 514–515].
  - At Oudenarde (1382) the Ghent army had an "abundance" of cannon and one huge bombard [text: Froissart (Johnes) Book II ch. CI, p. 710].
- **Saint-Sauveur-le-Vicomte (1375).** The coordinator named it as a gun siege. Johnes's text speaks only of "large engines" that "day and night cast stones into the castle". It never says cannon [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CCCXXII, pp. 505–506]. Guns there are **not confirmed** by the translation read.
- **Harfleur 1415.** Henry V used "the twelve great guns and other traditional artillery" [historian: Wikipedia *Siege of Harfleur*]. It was "the first time that an English army made significant use of gunpowder artillery in the siege of a large urban settlement" [same, citing Spencer 2017]. A 1415 royal record names at least five great guns: *Clysf*, *Fowler*, *Bristol*, *George* and *God's Grace* [historian: Spencer, agincourt600, citing The National Archives E 364].
- **Guns and engines together.** At Harfleur the defenders used "guns, catapults and engines" [historian: Spencer, paraphrasing the *Gesta*]. At Karlštejn (1422) the "catapults or large slings appear to have done more damage than the guns" [historian: Lützow p. 137]. **Ten Lion guns beside the trebuchets is right for the date.**

### 16b. What the guns looked like
- **Two ways to make a barrel** [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard (weapon)*]:
  - **Wrought iron, "hoop and stave":** iron bars laid lengthways round a core, with "iron hoops driven over them and welded hot". "The complicated procedure required a highly skilled forge."
    - Dulle Griet has **32 staves under 61 hoops** [historian: Wikipedia *Dulle Griet*].
    - Mons Meg has **25 staves** with hoops shrunk on [historian: relayed by search, Wikipedia *Mons Meg*].
    - **Look:** a dark iron tube banded all along with raised rings. This suits blocky low-poly very well.
  - **Cast bronze:** for example Faule Mette and the Grose Bochse (15th century) [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard*]. Small guns of our period are bronze: the Tannenberg handgonne is "cast bronze" [historian: Wikipedia *Hand cannon*], and the Loshult gun is a vase-shaped cast-bronze barrel (relayed).
- **Surviving guns from or near our period, with sizes:**

| Gun | Date | Make | Length | Bore | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Les Michelettes (gun 1) | c. 1400–1420 | wrought iron | 217 cm | 41 cm | Smith & Brown 1989, via Oriflamme |
| Les Michelettes (gun 2) | c. 1400–1420 | wrought iron | 364 cm | 51 cm | same. Left by the English after the 1423–24 siege of Mont-Saint-Michel |
| Bodiam moat bombard | late 14th / early 15th c. (relayed) | hoop and stave (relayed) | not found | about 30 cm | Wikipedia *Bombard*; search |

- **Later giants, for scale only.** Do not use these for 1400:
  - Pumhart von Steyr: early 15th century, 2.59 m, about 8 t, an 80 cm stone of 690 kg [historian: Wikipedia].
  - Dulle Griet: about 5 m, 16.4 t, 64 cm bore [historian: Wikipedia; date disputed, see 16i].
  - Mons Meg: 1449, 4.01 m, 5,080 kg, 49.6 cm [historian: Smith & Brown via Oriflamme].
- **Shape.** A wide barrel for the stone, with a **narrower powder chamber at the back**.
  - The Boxted bombard (c. 1450) has a chamber of 10.2 cm bore welded to the barrel (relayed, Wikipedia *Boxted Bombard*).
  - Mons Meg (1449) has a separate chamber that screws on (relayed).
  - Whether guns of **1380–1420** had separate removable, wedged-in chambers is [silent] in the sources read.
  - **Design:** a stubby banded barrel with a visibly narrower chamber section behind it, one piece.
- **Size range.** Bombards ran from "a meter in length" to "several meters long" [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard*]. Forteresse de Chinon calls its bombards and veuglaires "stubby guns" [historian: Chinon].
- **Small guns.**
  - A Western European handgun of 1380 "was fixed to a wooden pole" [historian: Wikipedia *Hand cannon*, gallery caption].
  - Handguns "could be held in two hands", or rested on a support [historian: same, citing Andrade 2016 p. 76].
  - The ribaudequin (several small barrels on one cart) was "mounted on wheels" by the 1380s [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].

### 16c. Mounting: beds, not wheels, for the big guns
- "The earliest guns sat directly on the ground, with earth piled under the muzzle." "As the size of guns increased, they began to be attached to heavy wooden frames or beds that were held down by stakes." These "began to be replaced by wheeled carriages in the early 16th century" [historian: Wikipedia *Gun carriage*].
- Elevation was set with **timber wedges** (later called the quoin) [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*; *Gun carriage*].
- **An in-period picture:** a trestle-mounted bombard in the *Grandes Chroniques de France*, 1390–1405 [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard*, image caption]. The picture itself was not seen.
- **Wheels for small pieces only:**
  - ribaudequins on wheels by the 1380s [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*];
  - "2-wheeled gun-carriages" at the siege of Quero in 1376 (relayed, an Italian source);
  - the first true wheeled travelling carriages in France in 1461, and Burgundian carriages captured in the 1470s (relayed).
- **Recoil.** The recoil of the largest guns smashed the beams behind them and needed repairs after shots (relayed, popular source).
- **Moving them.** Great guns needed "as many as three dozen horses and oxen" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*]. Mons Meg was moved on a cart (relayed). Guns travelled on wagons and were set into beds at the battery.
- **Design:** each big Lion gun lies in a **heavy timber trough or bed on the ground**, with stakes and a timber backstop behind it, and a wedge under the front. **No wheels and no trunnions.** Smaller guns may be on light two-wheeled carts (ribaudequin) or on wooden poles.

### 16d. Screens
- Guns "were fired at ground level behind a hinged timber shutter" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*, as returned by the fetch tool].
- Chinon's reconstructed bombard carriages "protected the gunners from the attackers" [historian: Forteresse de Chinon].
- No 1380–1420 picture of such a shutter was confirmed [silent; gap]. A 1529 print shows a gun behind a wooden wall (relayed).
- **Design:** a plank screen as tall as a man in front of each gun, propped from behind, with its top half hinged to lift up for the shot. Wicker or earth fill (gabions) is [silent].

### 16e. Crew and roles
- "The master gunner was usually the same person as the caster." Larger trains had "journeymen, smiths, carpenters, rope makers and carters" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].
- The Pumhart von Steyr needed a crew of **4–6** and had no sights [historian: Wikipedia *Pumhart von Steyr*].
- Handguns: one man, or two with a helper to light it [historian: Wikipedia *Hand cannon*].
- **Design:** per big gun, a master gunner plus 3–5 helpers and a carpenter at the bed. Per small gun, 1–2. Gunners are craftsmen, not knights: working clothes, perhaps a leather apron [INVENTED; the clothing is silent].

### 16f. Loading, aiming, firing
- **Muzzle-loaded.** "Most medieval cannon were muzzleloaders" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].
- **Tools:** "a copper scoop, a ramrod and a sponge". "A bucket of water was always kept beside the cannon." "Hot coals were used to heat the shot or keep the wire primer going." [historian: same]
- **Ignition:** through a **touch hole**, with "smoldering wood, coal, red-hot iron rods, or slow-burning matches" [historian: Wikipedia *Hand cannon*, citing Andrade 2016 p. 76]. The Tannenberg gun's flash pan still held powder when found (relayed).
- **Aiming:** by turning the bed and driving wedges. The Pumhart has "no sights" [historian: Wikipedia].
- **The c. 1420 *Firework Book* (Feuerwerkbuch)** gives the period's own loading method, but it was **not read** (see 16j).
- **Sequence for the scene** [historian: the tools above; the order is inferred, and the Feuerwerkbuch details are unverified]:
  1. Sponge the barrel with water.
  2. Scoop powder into the chamber and ram it.
  3. Roll in the stone ball and wedge it tight.
  4. Set the elevation wedge and lift the shutter.
  5. Crew steps aside; the master gunner touches a glowing iron rod (kept hot in a brazier of coals) to the touch hole.
  6. Boom, a flash and a cloud of smoke; the gun jolts back against its stakes.
  7. Shutter down; sponge; repeat.

### 16g. Rate of fire
- **Karlštejn, 1422:** the "Pražska" and "Jaroměřská" "could be fired seven times a day, and even the "Rychlice," famed for its rapidity, only thirty times." "The artillery fire ... was very slack." [historian: Lützow, *The Hussite Wars*, p. 137, Wikisource]
- The largest bombards might fire "as little as five times a day" (relayed, popular source).
- At Crécy (1346) Froissart reports the English guns making "two or three discharges" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].
- **Design:** realistically each gun fires **once in the 3-minute loop, perhaps twice for a small one**. Ten guns firing in a ragged stagger (one boom every 15–20 seconds across the battery) is already far faster than history. Say so in DESIGN.md's last section.

### 16h. What they shot, and what it did
- **Ammunition:**
  - Bombards fired "round stone projectiles" [historian: Wikipedia *Bombard*]. The Metz gun fired 6 kg stones.
  - Bore sizes suggest stones of about **30 cm** (the Bodiam gun) to **41–51 cm** (the Michelettes) [historian: the bores above; the stone size is inferred from the bore].
  - Handguns shot lead balls or arrows. A Loshult replica sent arrows 205–360 m and lead balls about 630 m [historian: McLachlan 2011 via Wikipedia *Hand cannon*].
  - **Design:** grey dressed stone balls piled by each big gun.
- **Effects:**
  - **Oudenarde 1382:** Ghent's bombard "shot stones of an immense weight". The garrison "comforted each other as well as they could, and defended themselves". No wall damage is described [text: Froissart (Johnes) Book II ch. CI, p. 710].
  - **Harfleur 1415:** "really fine buildings, almost as far as the middle of the town" were "totally demolished or threatened with inevitable collapse" [text: *Gesta*, Taylor & Roskell pp. 38–41, via Spencer]. The walls were "seriously damaged" [historian: Wikipedia]. The defenders kept firing from "shattered openings in walls" and repaired by night with timber, earth and stones [text: *Gesta*, quote and paraphrase via Spencer].
  - **Karlštejn 1422:** little effect (above).
  - Early cannon "affected morale more than they damaged buildings" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].
  - **Design:** a gunstone strike chips and cracks the wall face, bursts a merlon, or smashes a roof. That fits the scripted battlement break as well as a trebuchet stone does (§6b). **Night repairs with timber and earth** are an attested follow-up the Bulls could be shown doing.

### 16i. Smoke and sound
- **Sound.** Oudenarde: "it might be heard five leagues off in the day-time, and ten at night" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 710]. Chapter CVII gives "upwards of six leagues" [text: same, p. 717]. Villani: the guns "made a noise like thunder" [historian: quoted in Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*].
- **Smoke.** No source read describes the smoke's colour or size [silent]. A white-grey cloud rolling from the muzzle and drifting downwind is the natural choice. Mark it [INVENTED] and check it with the family.
- **Sound design:** a deep thunder-like boom with a long rolling tail. Keep it soft enough for a family TV.

### 16j. Frightening material (cannon)
1. **Karlštejn:** besiegers threw "large stink-pots and baskets containing ordure" to spread sickness, and the defenders' "teeth fall out" [historian: Lützow p. 137]. **Leave it out.**
2. Early guns were "probably as dangerous to their users as to the enemy" [historian: Wikipedia *Cannon in the Middle Ages*]. Mons Meg's barrel burst in 1660 [historian: Oriflamme]. **No bursting guns.**
3. Villani: the guns "caused much loss in men and horses" [historian: Wikipedia]. **No gunstone lands near a person or horse.**
4. Tannenberg's keep was destroyed by a gunpowder explosion in 1399 (relayed). **No explosions of buildings.**
5. Oudenarde: "it seemed as if all the devils in hell had broken loose" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 710]. **Don't put this on a label for 8–10-year-olds.**
6. Loud sudden booms can startle young children. Keep the levels moderate and give each boom a visible lead-up (the gunner's glowing iron) so it is never a surprise.

### 16k. Disagreements (cannon)
- **Dulle Griet's date:** "first half of the 15th century" (Wikipedia infobox), "late 15th century" (body text), "1382" (Manucy), c. 1450 (Smith & Brown). Its ball: 330 kg, 340 kg, or 320 kg (700 lb).
- **Ghent's 1382 bombard vs Dulle Griet:** Froissart's 1382 Oudenarde bombard and Dulle Griet (used by Ghent at Oudenaarde in 1452) are separate events. Do not merge them.
- **Froissart's "fifty feet in length"** (Johnes p. 710) is far beyond any surviving gun (the longest listed here is about 5 m). A search summary claimed a "fifty-three feet" reading, which was not found. Treat the length as exaggeration or a translation problem.
- **Bodiam bombard's date:** "late 14th century" versus "late 14th or very early 15th century" versus a Wars of the Roses dumping (all relayed).
- **Wheels:** small pieces had wheels from the 1370s–1380s, but heavy guns stayed in beds until the 1460s–1500s. Both are true, for different guns.

### 17b. The night sally to burn the ram

**Real models**
- **Berwick 1319: the closest match of all.** The English "made a sow that had a strong covering without and many armed men within" [text: Barbour p. 302]. "The besiegers in great array pushed their sow towards the wall" [p. 303]. A stone from the defenders' engine "hit the sow in such fashion that it broke in sunder with its dint the main beam" [p. 304]. "The men within the sow ran out with the greatest speed" [p. 304]. Then "John Crab then ... set fire to his faggots, and hurled them over the wall, and burnt the sow to bare firebrands" [p. 304]. **This burning happened by day, during an assault, from the wall. It was not a night sally.**
- **Vegetius IV.18: the night version, in the manual every medieval commander knew.**
  - Two methods are given. First, if there is courage enough, "eruptione facta globus egreditur armatorum" (a sally is made and a band of armed men goes out). They drive off the enemy and burn the great engine, "dereptis coriis de lignis exurit" (tearing the hides off the timber) [text: Vegetius IV.18; the Latin library's text reads "aramatorum"].
  - Second, "Depositi quoque homines funibus, cum hostes dormiunt, in lanternis portant lucernas": men let down on ropes while the enemy sleeps carry lamps inside lanterns. They set the engines alight and are drawn back up the wall ("incensis machinis") [text: Vegetius IV.18].
  - **Design note:** the family's "few Bulls slip out of the back gate at night" is Vegetius's first method in small numbers. The fire in a covered lantern is his second, and it suits a gentle night scene (a hooded glow moving in the dark).
- **Aiguillon 1346: a sally that destroyed works.** The French built a bridge with "three hundred workmen ... who worked day and night". The garrison "prepared three vessels, in which they embarked". Driving away the workmen and guards, they "instantly destroyed what had taken so much time to make" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CXX, p. 149]. The text does not say whether this was by night.
- **Berwick 1319: a sally to burn a ship.** "There sallied forth to her from the town a right great company, and presently they set her on fire" [text: Barbour p. 298]. Some of the crew were slain (see 17h).
- **Hennebont 1342** (already in §6d): Manny's sally broke the great engine by day. The countess burned an empty camp by day.
- **No chronicle read here describes a night sally that burned a ram.** The family's beat is a composite: the Vegetius method, Berwick's burnt sow, and the Hennebont and Aiguillon sallies. Mark it **[INVENTED, on these models]**.

**How fire was carried** [text unless marked]
- Berwick: "pitch and tar, with lint and hards and brimstone, and dry sticks that would burn easily", "made of them great faggots girded with broad bands of iron" [Barbour p. 302]. They were swung out "in a blazing bundle by means of their crane", a "high crane running on wheels" [p. 302].
- Vegetius: the falarica's head is wrapped with "sulphure resina bitumine stuppisque ... infusa oleo" (sulphur, resin, bitumen and tow, soaked in oil). "Malleoli uelut sagittae sunt", and they set alight whatever they stick to [IV.18]. These are fire arrows; see 17h.
- Lamps in lanterns, for the men let down at night [Vegetius IV.18].
- Torches as signals: at Oudenarde (1382) Sir Daniel de Haluin "hoisted above the walls" four torches "as a signal that the siege would be raised", "late at night" [text: Froissart (Johnes) Book II ch. CXXV, p. 746].
- Fire-pots: [silent] in everything read.
- **Design:** one Bull carries a hooded lantern [Vegetius], and another carries a bundle of tow and sticks [Barbour's materials, small]. They touch the bundle to the ram's roof and go back. **No fire arrows.**

**How engines were protected, and how fires were put out**
- **Raw hides.** Sheds: "crudis ac recentibus coriis uel centonibus operitur" ("covered with raw, fresh hides or patchwork quilts") so that fire thrown at them won't burn them [text: Vegetius IV.15]. Towers likewise [IV.17]. Rams [§4e above, Vegetius IV.14]. That is why the burning party first tears the hides off [IV.18].
- **Putting the fire out.** Vegetius gives water poured from above a gate onto a fire: "aqua subiectum restinguat incendium" [text: IV.4]. That is the defenders' method, not the besiegers'. At Berwick the defenders "with a force of men scattered the fire" set at their gate [text: Barbour p. 306]. **Besiegers putting out a burning engine is [silent]** in all the sources read.
- **Vinegar** appears in Vegetius only as siege stores ("uini aceti ... copiae", IV.7), never as a fire-fighter. Vinegar against fire is [silent] here (see 17j).
- **Earth:** [silent].
- **Design:** Lion guards wake, run up with buckets and beat the flames with cloaks or hides. The ram is scorched and partly burned, and it is repaired or replaced next day. [INVENTED in detail; the water is grounded in Vegetius IV.4.]

**Guarding engines and the camp at night**
- "Reginald" was paid for guarding the Warwolf's beams "for forty nights" [historian: Wikipedia *Warwolf*, citing Simpson & Galbraith vol. 5 p. 201; also §2 above].
- At Hennebont "those who guarded the machine fled" [text: §4f].
- At Berwick the besiegers "had so enclosed their host with trenches that they were strongly fortified" [text: Barbour p. 295]. After the assault the defenders "quickly appointed watches, and went to their quarters" [p. 307].
- At Oudenarde 1382, "Those of the guard in front of the camp armed themselves" when an alarm came [text: Froissart (Johnes) Book II ch. CXXI].
- Vegetius on the defenders' side: shelters on the walls in which "uigiles hibernis mensibus ab imbri uel frigore" (the watchmen, in winter, keep out of the rain and cold) [IV.26]. Defenders keep "acerrimos ac sagacissimos canes in turribus" (the fiercest, keenest-nosed dogs in the towers). "Anseres quoque ... nocturnos superuentus clamoribus indicant" (geese, too, give warning of night raids by their cries) [IV.26]. **A dog on the Bull tower that barks, or geese in the courtyard, is a gentle, sourced night tap moment.**
- Engines working through the night: at Hennebont the great machine "never ceased from casting stones into the town" "all that night" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 108]. At Aiguillon the twelve engines "cast stones into the fortress day and night" [ch. CXX, p. 149].
- Night repairs by defenders with timber, earth and stones: Harfleur [§16h].
- Watch fires, cooking fires and sentries walking rounds in the besiegers' camp are [silent] in the passages read. Light them as the night's warm points [INVENTED].

In the 1359 army every cart was "drawn by four good and strong horses" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CCX, p. 279]. Oxen on the trebuchet carts are plausible but [INVENTED]. ### 17d. Filling the moat to bring the ram across

- **Vegetius IV.16: the musculi.** These are small sheds under which soldiers "fossatum etiam adportatis lapidibus lignis ac terra non solum conplent" (not only fill the ditch with carried stones, timber and earth), and also make it firm, so that towers can be brought up [text].
- **Vegetius IV.15: the cover.** Vineae are covered sheds of light timber and hides. Plutei are mobile shields on wheels [text: Vegetius IV.15, as summarised by the fetch tool]. These are the Roman names for the mantlets and pavises of §4c.
- **Berwick 1319:** the English "quickly filled the ditches, and boldly advanced with their ladders to the walls" [text: Barbour p. 296]. With what, the passage read doesn't say.
- **Carlisle 1315:** "great numbers of fascines of corn and herbage to fill the moat". It failed, and the wheeled bridges sank [text: Lanercost, §6c].
- **Ypres 1383:** "to have quantities of faggots made and collected, with which and earth they could fill up the ditches". Workmen cut wood "in the neighbourhood of Ypres", but it "was not so soon done" and was never finished before the siege broke up [text: Froissart (Johnes) Book II ch. CLXX, vol. II p. 1].
- **Other Froissart cases** (relayed by search, pages not read): Scots levelling ditches with trees and beams so that machines could advance; Flemings at Oudenarde collecting "faggots and straw" for ditches "wide and full of water".
- **Château Gaillard:** "first filled the ditch" [historian: §6c].
- **How long:** no figure in any source read [silent]. Ypres shows it was slow, and that a siege could end before it was done.
- **Bodiam caveat:** its moat is wide water, 2 m or less deep (§3, §12). Filling a 30–50 m stretch is far more than any account here. Filling a **narrow strip beside the bridge or a corner** is the believable version.
- **Design:** pavise-bearers stand in front. Lions carry faggots (bundles of brushwood) and baskets of earth down to the water's edge and tip them in, under a roofed shed or behind a line of pavises. A path of bundles and earth grows out into the moat. The ram goes along it on planks [INVENTED in layout; methods from Vegetius IV.15–16, Ypres and Carlisle].

### 17f. "Everything at once": general assaults

- **Caerlaverock 1300.** "Then might be seen stones, arrows, and quarrels to fly among them". "Many ran there, many leaped there". "Those within, who were fully expecting it, bent their bows and crossbows". The fight lasted "all that day and night, and the next day until tierce" [text: Caerlaverock]. Engines, archers, crossbows and men at the walls, together.
- **Aiguillon 1346.**
  - Four divisions took turns: "the first of which should make an attack on this fortress from the dawn until about nine o'clock", then the next, and so on. "This mode of attack was continued for six successive days" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CXX, p. 149].
  - Assaults "two or three times every day" [p. 149].
  - Four towers were built "on the decks of four large vessels" [p. 150].
  - **Waves**, as the family asked, are sourced here.
- **Berwick 1319.** "To each lord and his battle was appointed a place where he should attack" [text: Barbour p. 295]. "To each battlement archers were assigned to shoot" [p. 296]. All at once: ditches filled, ladders, a ship by sea, then the sow on a second assault. At the Marygate the attackers "hewed down the barrier" and "made a fire at the drawbridge" [p. 306].
- **Ypres 1383.** "there was one much severer than the rest; for it lasted from morning until almost night". Archers "shot so expertly and rapidly that scarcely any dared to appear on the battlements" [text: Froissart (Johnes) vol. II p. 1].
- **Guns with engines:** Harfleur and Karlštejn [§16a].
- **Design:** day two's great assault is sourced as a set of sectors, each attacked at once (Berwick), in waves (Aiguillon), lasting most of the day (Ypres).

### 17g. The rhythm of a two-day siege

- **Dawn starts.**
  - Aiguillon's assaults began "from the dawn" [text: p. 149].
  - Brother Robert shot "from the dawn of the preceding day until the evening" [text: Caerlaverock].
  - Armies decamp "at break of day" or "before sunrise" [text: Froissart (Johnes) pp. 160, 171; Pasquerel p. 289].
- **Night ends the assault.**
  - "When night fell the English host all withdrew from the attack, wounded, weary, and hard stricken" [text: Barbour p. 307].
  - Ypres' great assault ran "from morning until almost night" [text: vol. II p. 1].
- **But engines could shoot all night:** Hennebont, Aiguillon (17b).
- **Caerlaverock fought through a night:** "all that day and night" [text].
- **After the fight.** Defenders "appointed watches, and went to their quarters" [text: Barbour p. 307]. At Bourbourg (1383) the French "retired to their quarters, to attend the sick and bury the dead" (relayed by search; page not read).
- **Pauses.** Orléans: the fighting was set aside so troops could "honor the feast-day in peace" [historian: Wikipedia, citing Ramsay p. 393]. And the Sunday standoff above.
- **Truces to collect the wounded:** not found in any source read [silent]. Froissart has many truces during sieges (Tournai, three days), but those found were for negotiation (relayed by search).
  - **Design, gently:** at dusk on day one, horns sound, both sides stop, and the fallen are helped or carried back to their own lines. Nobody is shown hurt. This is [INVENTED], modelled on Barbour and Bourbourg.
- **Night jobs:** wall repairs (Harfleur, §16h), guarding beams (Warwolf), watches (Berwick), dogs and geese (Vegetius IV.26), night signals (Oudenarde's torches), a night ride past the enemy camp (the countess left "about midnight" and came "about sun-rise, riding along one of the sides of the enemy's host" [text: Froissart (Johnes) p. 106]).

### 17h. Frightening material (two-day version)

**Leave out:**
1. **Fire on people.** Vegetius's malleoli and falaricae: fire arrows soaked in oil and sulphur [IV.18]. **No fire arrows at all.** Greek fire was not found in any source read; leave it out too.
2. **Aiguillon:** "The besieged flung down upon them stones, hot lime, large beams, and boiling water" [text: p. 150].
3. **Aiguillon's parting sally** "cut down and slew numbers" [p. 171]. Show a chase only.
4. **Berwick's ship:** the crew were partly slain [text: Barbour p. 298]. The English withdrew "wounded, weary, and hard stricken" [p. 307].
5. **Caerlaverock:** "did not flinch until some of them were slain" [text]. Bourbourg: "bury the dead" (relayed).
6. **Damazan:** the garrison was "put to the sword" [Froissart (Johnes), p. 149 area].
7. **Men inside the burning sow.** At Berwick they ran out before the fire [p. 304]. **Keep that order:** the ram is empty before any flame. In the night sally nobody is in the ram, and the guards wake only after the Bulls are gone.
8. **A burning drawbridge and gate** (Berwick p. 306): fine at a distance, but don't add it as a further fire.

**Fine:** a small scorched fire on an empty ram roof; lanterns; geese and dogs raising the alarm; leafy huts; carts; earth baskets; the Lions packing and leaving; "let them depart".

### 17i. Disagreements

- **Built on site, or carried in parts?** Turnbull (via Wikipedia) says counterweight trebuchets "had to be constructed close to the site". Other sources say a master rebuilt them "after they were deconstructed for transportation" (Wikipedia note 117). Caerlaverock has engines arriving by sea *and* new ones erected on site. Both happened. The Warwolf was built at Stirling (Prestwich).
- **The Warwolf's crew and time:** "five master carpenters and forty-nine" for "at least three months" (Prestwich), versus "50 workmen" (HES blog). It may have been finished only after the surrender (Prestwich p. 502).
- **The 30 wagons:** citation needed (Wikipedia). Don't use the figure.
- **Bothwell's belfry:** built at Glasgow and hauled on a corduroy road (Pilling, no source given), or simply "hauled from Glasgow" (HES).
- **Hennebont page numbers:** p. 106 or 107 for the same lines, depending on the reading (§6d vs 17e).
- **Who burned the sow at Berwick:** Barbour credits Crab's faggots, after an engine stone had already broken it. A secondary site says the fire was "lowered from the walls" (relayed). Barbour's text says "hurled them over the wall".

### 18b. The way up: stairs and the wall-walk

- **Newel stairs in the towers.** The towers' "walls and stone newel stairways survive largely intact" [historian: Gatehouse, from the scheduling report]. The listing gives the corner towers stair "vices" [historian: Historic England listing 1044134, **relayed**; the page itself returned 403].
- "You could climb the steep spiral staircases to the top of the towers" [historian: NT]. The postern tower's stair is "steep with narrow treads and a steep rise" [historian: NT]. The visitor access statement gives up to 56 steps to a tower top [**relayed**; the PDF could not be read].
- **Gatehouse.** The small turrets on the gate towers are "only heads to the stair-wells" [historian: History Today, **relayed**; the page returned 403].
- **Up to the parapet.** One can "climb up an internal stair to the parapet walk in several places", and reach "the roof of the Postern Tower" [historian: Britain Express].
- **A stair inside the range.** A newel stair in the south range linked the lord's chambers to the great hall; only its base remains [historian: Time Travel Britain; weak source].
- **Whether the wall-walk ran all the way round, or passed through the towers:** [silent]. No source read says.

### 18d. The rooms in a siege (sourced only)

- **The well.** Vegetius IV.10: "Magna urbis utilitas est, cum perennes fontes murus includit" ("It is a great advantage when the wall encloses perennial springs") [text: Vegetius IV.10]. Bodiam's well is spring-fed (18c).
- **Stores.** Vegetius IV.8 says to lay in pitch, sulphur and oil "to burn the enemy's engines"; iron and charcoal for making weapons; and timber for spear shafts and arrows [text: Vegetius IV.8]. Bodiam had cellars along the east side and under each corner tower, and storage under the north range [historian: Northwestern; Thackray p. 40]. What they actually held is [silent].
- **Shelter under bombardment.** At Auberoche the garrison hid in "the vaulted rooms on the ground floor" [text: Froissart (Johnes) ch. CVI; see §6b].
- **Women carrying stones up to the walls:** Hennebont [text: §2].
- **The hall used as a barracks:** [silent] in every source read. The garrison had its own living area at Bodiam (Thackray p. 40), not the great hall.
